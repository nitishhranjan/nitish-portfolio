How I Think

The product habits I rely on most

These are the patterns that show up repeatedly in the work below, whether the product is client-facing, internal, or still in early exploration.

1. Find the real bottleneck

Do not confuse data availability with decision usability

Many workflows already have data somewhere. The harder problem is making it easy for the right user to ask the right question and trust the answer quickly.

2. Build for trust

Especially when the product output can influence action

I care about interpretability, grounding, constraints, and what can go wrong in the user workflow. In AI-heavy products, credibility is part of the feature set.

3. Scope with intent

v1 should unlock the core behavior, not imitate the full vision

The strongest evidence of product judgment is often what you left out, why you left it out, and how that made adoption more likely.

Internal product · 0 to 1

LLM self-service analytics platform

Built a natural-language analytics workflow for internal teams who needed charts, distributions, and quick answers from standard analytics tables without writing SQL or waiting on analyst support.

RAG LangGraph Internal tooling Adoption
Primary userProject managers, CSMs, and operations stakeholders
Decision supportedCan I answer this question and present it without going through the analyst queue?
RoleProduct owner, end to end
Outcome100 non-technical users onboarded

The product problem

AiDash already had standard analytics tables, but non-technical teams still could not use them directly. For simple exploratory questions, distribution checks, or customer-facing visualizations, they either had to write queries they were not comfortable with or wait on a BA to pull and present the answer.

What I optimized for

The goal was not "add LLMs to analytics." It was to reduce dependency on analysts for lightweight questions while keeping the experience trustworthy enough that PMs and CSMs could use it in real conversations.

What I chose and what I rejected

Static dashboards were too rigid for ad-hoc questions. SQL training would only help a small subset of users. Keeping BA support as the default path did not scale. I chose a self-service workflow that grounded natural-language questions on known tables and definitions, then returned both analysis and visual output in one place.

How I scoped v1

v1 focused on internal usage only: natural-language analytics over standard tables, chart generation, and direct visual exploration without BI tooling. I intentionally did not push immediately into client-facing release, because trust guardrails, permissions, and multi-tenant concerns needed a tighter product pass first.

What happens next

Post-v1, the product path is to embed the same self-service analytics capability inside the SaaS platform for clients, where the challenge shifts from adoption to governance, entitlement, and answer correctness at customer scale.

Personal project · 0 to 1

Locality Lens

Designed a location-intelligence product that turns raw OpenStreetMap data into personalized neighborhood analysis for people evaluating where to live, relocate, or invest.

Geospatial AI LangGraph Groq + OSM 3 to 5s
Primary userSomeone comparing neighborhoods for lifestyle fit
Decision supportedIs this locality right for me?
Core insightUsers need interpretation, not just POI counts
Outcome46 metrics narrowed to 5 to 8 relevant ones per user

The product problem

People deciding where to live usually bounce between maps, listing sites, commute estimates, and vague online opinions. The real decision is lifestyle fit, not raw place count. I wanted a product that could translate open geospatial data into a user-specific answer.

What I chose and why

A rules-only score would have been fast but too rigid. An unconstrained LLM summary over all metrics would be slow, noisy, and expensive. I used the LLM earlier in the workflow to infer user intent and select only the 5 to 8 metrics that mattered from a catalog of 46, then grounded the summary in structured statistics.

Performance tradeoff that mattered

I consolidated many category-specific OpenStreetMap calls into one optimized fetch with in-memory classification, cutting API calls by 83 percent and keeping response time in the 3 to 5 second range.

What shipped

A Streamlit product with location input, optional user profile, personalized summary, quick stats, detailed metrics, and an interactive map. LangGraph handled validation, intent extraction, geocoding, OSM fetch, metric calculation, and summary generation through a single orchestrated flow.

Personal project · 0 to 1

RAG document QA system

Built a document assistant for people who need reliable answers from dense PDFs without manually reading, cross-searching, and stitching context themselves.

Hybrid retrieval Citations AWS EC2 t3.micro
Primary userSomeone extracting trustworthy answers from long PDFs
Decision supportedCan I trust this answer quickly enough to act?
ConstraintLow-cost deployment with 1 GB RAM
OutcomeImmediate base-corpus startup plus runtime PDF upload

The product problem

Long technical PDFs are hard to interrogate. Users end up keyword-searching, opening multiple sections, and manually combining fragments into one answer. The product challenge was to make this workflow faster without asking users to blindly trust the model.

What I chose and what I rejected

Dense-only retrieval was simpler but weak on exact phrases and terminology. Keyword-only search was better on literal matches but brittle on paraphrased questions. I combined vector search with BM25, added follow-up question condensation, and made citations part of the product experience so users could verify answers quickly.

Constraint-driven product choice

The system needed to run on a low-cost AWS EC2 t3.micro instance. I committed a pre-built vector index with the repo so users did not wait through a heavy cold-start indexing flow, and I allowed runtime PDF upload to extend the session without rebuilding the base corpus.

What I would do next

The next step would be a formal evaluation layer for retrieval hit rate, citation precision, answer faithfulness, and latency across document types, so quality becomes measurable rather than just subjectively good.
