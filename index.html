User problem

PMs and CSMs needed distributions, trends, and customer-ready views without writing SQL or waiting in the analyst queue.

Product decision

Ship an internal natural-language analytics workflow over approved tables before attempting a client-facing SaaS rollout.

Business outcome

100 non-technical users adopted v1, moving lightweight analytics from request queues to faster self-service exploration.

The problem

We already had standard analytics tables, but non-technical teams could not use them directly. PMs and CSMs still waited on business analysts for distributions, trends, and customer-ready views. The problem was not lack of data; it was the gap between available data and usable decision support.

Constraints

The tool had to sit on existing analytics tables, answer ad-hoc questions, and produce outputs usable in internal reviews and customer conversations. Accuracy and interpretability mattered more than novelty, because a wrong chart is worse than no chart. We also had to ship internally first while keeping a path open for a future client-facing rollout.

What I considered — and what I killed

Static dashboards worked for recurring metrics but failed on exploratory cuts and one-off customer questions. SQL training helped only a small subset of users, and keeping the BA queue as the default path did not scale. I chose a self-service workflow because users could ask in natural language, stay grounded in known tables and definitions, and get analysis plus visualization in one experience.

What shipped in v1 — and what I left out

V1 focused on natural-language querying over standard analytics tables, chart and distribution generation, and outputs PMs and CSMs could use without SQL or BI tooling. I kept the first release internal to validate usefulness and question patterns before taking on client-grade concerns like tenant isolation, permissions, and deeper SaaS integration.

Results

100 non-technical users across customer success and operations now query product data without SQL or BI tools. Analytics moved from a queue-based service model to a conversational workflow where teams can inspect distributions, generate visuals, and get to a usable answer faster on their own. Post-v1, the next step under consideration is extending this into the SaaS product for clients.

What I'd do differently

If I were taking it to the next stage, I would formalize evaluation and trust guardrails earlier, especially for chart correctness, business-definition consistency, and permission boundaries. Those become much more important once the product moves from an internal enablement tool to something exposed inside a client-facing SaaS workflow.

The problem

Someone evaluating a neighborhood usually has to jump between listing sites, maps, commute estimates, and vague online opinions. The real question is not "how many places are nearby?" but "is this area a fit for my lifestyle?" I wanted to build a decision tool that could answer that for different users, whether they were a student, a family, or a working professional.

Constraints

The product had to stay low-cost by relying on open data, work across cities with uneven map quality, and feel fast enough to be interactive. That meant minimizing data-fetch calls, handling invalid inputs and geocoding failures gracefully, and avoiding a wall of generic metrics that would make the output technically rich but practically useless.

What I considered — and what I killed

A rules-only scoring system would have been fast, but too rigid for nuanced user needs. On the other hand, sending all available data to an LLM for an open-ended summary would feel smart but create latency, noise, and higher cost. I used the LLM earlier in the workflow to extract intent and select only the 5-8 most relevant metrics from a catalog of 46, then grounded the final summary in structured statistics. I also replaced many category-specific OSM queries with one consolidated fetch plus in-memory classification, which cut API calls by 83% and made the experience faster and cheaper.

What shipped

A Streamlit app where a user enters a locality and optional profile, then gets a personalized summary, quick stats, a detailed metrics view, and an interactive map of nearby POIs. Under the hood, LangGraph orchestrates validation, intent extraction, geocoding, OSM fetch, statistical analysis, and summary generation with conditional routing, caching, and parallel execution.

What I'd do differently

I would add comparison mode and feedback capture next. The current product is strong at answering "what is this area like?" for one place at a time; the next version should help users compare two or three localities side by side and learn from whether the recommendation actually matched their decision.

"Can I trust this answer quickly enough to act without reading the whole document?"
Timeline
Nov 2025 – Dec 2025
Stack
Hybrid retrieval (dense + BM25), multi-turn Q&A, AWS EC2, Docker
Outcome
Shipped a usable PDF-Q&A app that runs on an AWS EC2 t3.micro, loads a pre-indexed corpus instantly, and lets users extend the knowledge base with new PDFs during a live session.
The problem

Long technical PDFs are hard to interrogate. A user usually has to search keywords, open multiple pages, and stitch together an answer manually. I wanted a lightweight product that could answer natural-language questions over a document set, preserve conversational flow for follow-ups, and still show the source pages so the user could verify what the model said.

Constraints

The app had to run on low-cost infrastructure, specifically an AWS EC2 t3.micro instance with 1 GB RAM, while staying responsive enough for chat. That meant avoiding a heavy deployment footprint, preventing cold-start indexing on deploy, and reducing hallucination risk enough that source citations were a core part of the experience rather than a nice-to-have.

What I considered — and what I killed

A dense-only retriever was simpler but unreliable on exact phrases, acronyms, and terminology-heavy questions that are common in PDFs. A keyword-only approach handled exact matches better but broke on paraphrased queries. I combined vector search with BM25 so the product could handle both semantic and literal lookup, then added question condensation for follow-ups so retrieval stayed strong across multi-turn conversations. I also committed a pre-built vector index instead of reprocessing the corpus on every deploy, because faster startup was more valuable than a perfectly pure indexing flow for this use case.

Results

The result is a working document assistant that is immediately usable after deploy, supports runtime PDF uploads without rebuilding the base corpus, and makes answers easier to trust through page-level citations. The strongest product outcome was usability: it moved the experience from "RAG demo" to "tool someone can actually use to navigate a document set."

What I'd do differently

If I were taking it further, I would add an offline evaluation layer before expanding the feature set: retrieval hit-rate, citation precision, answer faithfulness, and upload latency by document type. The current version proves the workflow and deployment choices; the next step would be making quality measurable.
